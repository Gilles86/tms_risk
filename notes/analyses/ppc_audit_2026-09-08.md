# Audit: why the group-level PPCs look bad

Two separate things, one family-wide and one specific to the fit I had just
recommended.

## 1. The group PPCs are not bad — they are plotted at a resolution where
sampling noise dominates

I first read the ladder-rung residual as a slope misfit. It is mostly not.

**The decisive number.** Residual divided by the observed point's own standard
error, per cell:

| table | median |residual| / s.e.m. | coverage |
|---|---|---|
| `ppc_anchor.rung` (24 cells) | **0.97** | 79% |
| `ppc_anchor.stake3rung` (72 cells) | **0.73** | 88% |

A ratio of 1.0 means the residuals are exactly the size expected from sampling
noise in the observed proportions alone. At 0.97 and 0.73 there is essentially
nothing left for the model to have got wrong. Each plotted point is a
subject-averaged proportion over 12–20 trials per participant; its own s.e.m.
is 0.037–0.053, barely narrower than the model's predictive band. The dots
scatter around the curve by as much as they should, and the eye reads that as
misfit.

**Checked at full resolution too.** Ranking every trial within
(participant × safe payoff) gives a 48-point ladder instead of 6. Pooled over
that:

* residual trend **+0.0005 per rung** (+0.024 over the whole ladder)
* computed WITHIN participant and then averaged: **+0.0005 per rung**, so it is
  not an aggregation artefact either
* per-participant indifference point: SD observed 0.312, model **0.319**
  (ratio 1.02) — the model spreads participants correctly
* per-participant slope: observed 0.810, model **0.756**, r = **0.92**

**The one real thing.** That slope ratio is 0.93: the model's psychometric
function is about **7% too flat**. Over six rungs that shows up as the +0.016
to +0.026 per-rung drift seen in every fit, and it is why coverage is 79–88%
rather than 95% — the bands are slightly too narrow because the curve is
slightly too shallow. It is a limitation worth one sentence, not the failure it
looks like.

## 2. `--tau_intercept 0.10` bought convergence by breaking the model

`log-power-n1n2.mapjitter.klw.ti0.1` converges (r̂ 1.010, ESS 1214) and is the
**worst model in the set on every predictive metric**:

| | default prior | ti0.1 |
|---|---|---|
| mean residual | +0.007 | **+0.012** |
| RMSE | 0.053 | **0.063** |
| rung trend / rung | +0.017 | **+0.026** |
| coverage of the 95% band | 88% | **81%** |

Cause: `--tau_intercept` applies to `noise`, `prior_mu` **and** `prior_sd`. It
crushes the between-subject SD of the magnitude prior's location:

| parameter | default | ti0.1 | |
|---|---|---|---|
| `log_risky_prior_mu_sd` | 0.798 [0.515, 1.208] | 0.354 [0.264, 0.464] | −56% |
| `log_safe_prior_mu_sd` | 0.910 [0.534, 1.516] | 0.384 [0.280, 0.498] | −58% |
| `log_n2_power_sd7_sd` | 0.553 | 0.309 | −44% |

`fit_anchor.py`'s own PRIORS block says of `prior_mu`:

> *tau is LOOSE: measured tau is 0.60–0.72 and subject estimates use 85–87% of
> it, so this is the best-identified parameter in the model and must not be
> shrunk.*

Under the default prior the posterior (0.80 / 0.91) sits **above** the prior's
own scale (0.75) — what a well-identified parameter looks like. Under τ = 0.10
it is pinned at 0.35, so the prior is doing the work, not the data. Individual
differences in where each participant's magnitude prior sits ARE the funnel;
removing them removes the funnel, the fit, and the quantity the model exists to
estimate.

**`ti0.1` must not be the reported model.** Job 5659502, which was refitting the
whole ladder at that prior, is cancelled.

## The targeted fix

`--tau_noise` (new) tightens the noise anchors **only**, leaving `prior_mu` and
`prior_sd` at PRIOR_SPEC. That is where the funnel actually is: the worst-mixing
parameter is always `log_n1_power_sd7_sd` or `log_n2_power_sd112_sd`, a noise
group SD, never a prior one. Job 5660005 fits τ_noise 0.10/0.15 ×
target_accept 0.99/0.995.

If that converges, it is a defensible reported model. If it does not, the
choice is between a non-converged `n1n2` and a converged `perc`, and the
`ti0.1` shortcut is not a third option.

## Mean or median across participants? Mean, and it is not a close call

Every PPC in the repo collapses across participants with the **mean**, applied
identically to the observed and the simulated choices
(`extract_anchor_ppc.py:136-139`: subject-level proportion, then mean across
subjects, on both sides). `model_median` in `ppc_stats` is a median over
posterior DRAWS, which is a different axis and is the right summary there.

Swapping in the median across participants, on the same trace and the same
cells:

| order | stake | obs (mean) | model (mean) | obs (median) | model (median) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Risky first | 13 | +0.012 | −0.021 | **−0.050** | −0.022 |
| Risky first | 23 | −0.009 | −0.003 | 0.000 | +0.001 |
| Risky first | 42 | +0.016 | +0.015 | **−0.050** | +0.041 |
| Risky second | 13 | +0.060 | +0.025 | +0.100 | +0.036 |
| Risky second | 23 | +0.061 | +0.012 | +0.050 | +0.011 |
| Risky second | 42 | +0.037 | +0.021 | **−0.050** | −0.016 |

The median **flips the sign in three of six cells**, including risky-second at
42 CHF. The reason is quantisation, not skew: each participant contributes a
median of 20 trials to a cell, so a per-participant proportion moves in steps of
0.05, and the median of 35 such values lands on one of those steps. Every median
in the table above is an exact multiple of 0.05 — the estimator has thrown away
the resolution the effect lives in.

The mean is also what the model's own predictive distribution is a
distribution *of*, and what the hierarchical fits treat as the sampling unit.
`extract_anchor_mechanism.py` records the same conclusion from the other side:
the between-participant distribution is right-skewed enough that the median
compresses the risky-second effect by 40%.
